===== 01-abstract =====
For about a decade, researchers have tried to predict how much a company's stock
will move in the weeks after its quarterly earnings call, using what executives
say on the call and how they sound. Many papers report that such models beat
classical forecasts built from past price swings. We find that the shared test
files behind these claims cannot support them. The files leave no gap between the
training and test periods even though every target looks 30 days ahead, 44-80%
of their test calls come from companies the model also saw in training, and they
place "day zero" on the day of the call although most companies release their
numbers before the market opens that morning. We built ecvol-bench to fix this.
It adds company names and dates to a public audio corpus that ships without
them, times each earnings release from SEC filings, provides targets under both
horizon definitions in use, gives splits that keep training and test apart in
time and in company, and includes a set of identity checks that run on any file
of predictions. With these checks in place, modern open text and audio models add
nothing to a forecast built from past volatility alone. Text embeddings mostly
identify which company is talking (89.5% probe accuracy), audio features do no
better than a constant, and combining the two helps in one horizon out of eight.
When we rerun the field's own models on their own data, the text models lose
12-82% in accuracy once test companies are unseen, while price-only models do
not move. Scoring the frozen pipeline on 475 calls from late 2025, after every
model's training cutoff, changes none of this. All code, metadata, splits,
targets, features and result tables are released.

===== 02-intro-a =====
An earnings call is the one regular occasion on which a company's managers speak
at length about their business. Since [ref], a line of papers has
used both the transcript and the audio of these calls to predict how volatile the
stock will be over the following 3 to 30 trading days. HTML [ref],
MAEC [ref], VolTAGE [ref], DialogueGAT
[ref], KeFVP [ref] and ECC Analyzer
[ref] all report beating classical forecasts. Two findings cast doubt
on that. [ref] showed that transcript representations mostly tell the
model which company is speaking, and that a simple forecast from the
company's own past volatility beats every transcript model. A survey of financial
NLP found that only about 14% of reported results could be reproduced
[ref].

We think both problems trace back to the same place. Nearly every paper in this
line is scored on one set of label and split files, handed down from paper to
paper. We audited those files (Section N). They leave no gap between
training and test dates even though each target runs 30 days past its call. Most
test calls come from companies that are also in training, and no paper reports
what happens when they are not. They define horizons in calendar days, which later
papers describe as trading days. They put day 0 on the date of the call, although
most companies publish their results before the market opens that day. The only
open corpus with raw audio, FinCall-Surprise [ref], has no ticker,
date or speaker fields at all, so the checks that would expose the
company-identity problem cannot be run on it. Its successor, MM-FinEval
[ref], adds tickers and dates but none of the checks.

===== 03-intro-b =====
What we contribute. 
(1) ecvol-bench, with company identities and dates recovered for
FinCall-Surprise (92.9% of calls, and a 60-call manual audit passes 60/60),
release times from 8-K filings, three target families under both horizon
definitions, splits with a 30-session gap and company-disjoint splits, checksums
for every file, and a harness in which every table comes from one command
(Section N). (2) An audit of the shared label and split
files (Table N). (3) Identity checks (ticker probes,
same-company and random content swaps, company-disjoint evaluation), released as
a command that works on any prediction file. (4) Results for
modern open-weight text and audio models against strong baselines
(Section N). (5) A reproduction study of the five runnable
models in this line, on their own data, with the same checks, plus a
code-availability audit of seventeen papers. (6) A post-cutoff test of
the frozen pipeline on 475 calls from late 2025. Stages, checks and confirmatory
tests were fixed in a public decision log before any model was run.

Related work. 
Four known pitfalls shaped the design. The first is company-identity leakage
[ref], which language models reproduce even on transcripts with names
removed [ref]. The second is lookahead bias in language models,
for which diagnostics and cutoff-bounded models exist
[ref]. The third is weak baselines,
since persistence, GARCH and HAR-RV [ref] are hard to beat
[ref]. The fourth is gender bias in audio features
[ref]. [ref] time earnings releases from
8-K filings to predict returns, but nobody had audited the volatility label sets.
The strongest recent positive results [ref] report
no identity checks and release no code.

===== 04-bench-a =====
The EarningsCall (EC) benchmark of [ref] and the two MAEC cohorts
are scored on split and label files that VolTAGE and KeFVP redistribute. We
audited those files (Table N). The VolTAGE and KeFVP test files
are byte for byte the same file, so the whole line of work shares one label set
that was never recomputed from prices. Every boundary between training,
validation and test has a zero-day gap while the targets reach 30 days past each
call. On the time-ordered splits the field reports, 80.4% (EC), 44.2% (MAEC-15)
and 55.7% (MAEC-16) of test calls are from companies that also appear in
training. Any time-ordered split allows that. The problem is that no paper reports
the company-disjoint case, so the confound of [ref] has never been
measured on these benchmarks. The original paper defines its horizons in calendar
days, but most later work describes them as trading days (21 sessions rather than
30 at =30). Finally, the shipped MAEC labels correlate only 0.52-0.87 with
calendar-day targets we recompute from prices under the same call-date anchor
(0.25-0.66 at =3). We could not reproduce them under any single stated
rule.

Corpora and company identity. 
FinCall-Surprise [ref] is the primary corpus, with 2,688
calls from 2019-2021 and MP3 audio, transcripts and slides. It ships four fields
per record and no ticker, date, CIK or speaker names. We recover the company and
the date from the transcript text and the slide metadata, check them against price
data, and release the table with its 80 manual corrections. 2,496 calls resolve
(92.9% overall, 95.3% of the earnings-type calls), and a 60-call manual audit
passes 60/60. MAEC [ref] is the secondary corpus, with about
3,443 calls from 2015-2018, sentence-aligned audio features and no raw audio.
Earnings25 [ref] has 514 S&P 500 calls from
July-December 2025, with webcast MP3 at 64 kbps for 281 calls and at most
24 kbps for 232. It postdates every model's training data, and 475 of its calls
map to a ticker and join to prices.

===== 05-bench-b =====
Targets. 
Daily adjusted prices come from a free source and were checked against Tiingo on
50 tickers. Let rt be the return on session t, counted from day 0, the last
session before the call's information is public. The main target is
vpost()=1nt=1n(rt- r)2 for
\3,7,15,30\, and vpre() is the same quantity over the
sessions ending at day 0. Under the trading-day definition n=. Under
the calendar-day definition of [ref] the window holds every
session within calendar days, so n0.7, and at =3 a
Thursday or Friday call has at most one session. We drop any window with fewer
than two sessions and record the reason. That removes 42.5% of FinCall's calls at
=3 calendar days, for which the released label sets still carry a value.
The two definitions are not interchangeable. vpost correlates only
0.78 between them at =3, and the persistence MSE is 44-63% higher there
under calendar days, so a published MSE means little unless its definition is
stated (Appendix Table N). Every result below uses trading
days, and the definition is a column in every result table. We add two targets
that are harder to game with company identity. The change
 v=vpost-vpre subtracts the company's own level,
and the HAR-residual subtracts a HAR-RV forecast. After joining to
prices, FinCall has 2,433 calls with a target (97.5%) and MAEC 2,688 (78.6%,
the rest having been delisted).

===== 06-bench-c =====
Almost no transcript states a clock time, so we take the moment the information
became public from EDGAR, as the acceptance time of the earliest 8-K filing with
Item 2.02 (the earnings release) within three days before to one day after the
call. That covers 89.3% of FinCall, 68.6% of MAEC and 99.8% of Earnings25. The
rest fall back, in recorded order, to Earnings25's call-start time, the
before/after-market flag of [ref], and an after-hours assumption. A
release before the open makes the call date itself day 1. Compared with the
call-date anchor that every released label set uses, day 0 moves for 53%
of FinCall, 56% of MAEC and 72% of Earnings25 calls, almost always one session
earlier. Under the published anchor, the session in which the market reacts
therefore sits in the pre window for most calls. On the measured anchor the
persistence MSE is up to 11% higher on FinCall, 25% on MAEC and 27% on
Earnings25, and v gains a positive mean (on FinCall +0.52 at =3
and +0.10 at =30). The call-date targets ship as a labelled variant.

Splits and release. 
Temporal splits (about 70/10/20 in date order) leave a 30-trading-day gap
between segments so that no target window crosses a boundary.
ticker-disjoint splits keep every validation and test company out of
training. Their overlap is 0.0%, against 91.3% (FinCall) and 79.4% (MAEC) on
our own temporal splits. The harness fails any feature that reads price data after
the call's as-of time, and leakage assertions run in CI. The release contains all
of the above, the derived-feature archives, the harness, and every result CSV with
its run record (resolved config, git commit, seeds and file hash). Raw audio and
transcripts are never redistributed.

===== 07-setup-a =====
Models, from simplest to richest. 
We organise our own models in stages. Stage 0 holds the classical
forecasts, namely persistence ( v=vpre), the training-set mean
of the target (a constant), EWMA (=0.94), HAR-RV [ref]
and GARCH(1,1). Stage 1 is a LightGBM model on past-volatility features,
transcript length, number of Q&A turns and a company fixed effect, our
identity-only baseline. Stage 2 uses the transcript through BGE-M3
[ref] embeddings pooled per section (1024-d), FinBERT [ref]
sentiment aggregates and surface statistics. Stage 3 uses the audio,
resampled to 16 kHz mono, through eGeMAPSv02 functionals [ref] and
WavLM-Large [ref] and emotion2vec+ [ref] embeddings over
30-second windows, averaged per call. Stage 4 combines modalities by
gated fusion or by stacking (a ridge over the text, audio and Stage-1 predictions,
fit on the validation set). From Stage 2 on, every model is reported with and
without a block of past-volatility features (vpre at all horizons
plus the HAR-RV inputs). The prediction heads are a ridge regression, with its
penalty chosen on validation, and a small MLP run with five seeds. The MLPs are
unstable across seeds (standard deviation of R2 up to 6.8), so the main grid
uses one deterministic model per stage, chosen before the results were known. MLP
cells are in Appendix N.

Metrics and tests. 
We report MSE on log realised volatility and, as the headline metric, R2
relative to persistence [ref], defined as
1-(y- y)2/(y- ypersist)2. Because v has a
positive mean on the measured anchor, persistence (which predicts v=0)
is a weak reference there, so every v claim is also compared with the
constant model. Diebold-Mariano tests [ref] with the small-sample
correction compare per-call squared errors against persistence and against the
previous stage. The confirmatory comparisons (each stage against Stage 1 on
 v across horizons, Stage 4 against the best single modality, and the
published split against the controlled split for each reproduced model) are
Holm-corrected. All other analyses are exploratory.

===== 08-setup-b =====
We use four. (1) A company-only model predicts each company's training
mean. (2) A same-company swap gives each test call the transcript or audio
of another call by the same company, and a random swap gives it any other
call. A model that does not change under the first is reading identity, and one
that does not change under the second is reading nothing. (3) A linear
probe tries to recover the ticker from the frozen call embedding.
(4) Every table shows ticker-disjoint results next to temporal ones. The command
ecvol audit predictions runs the prediction-side versions of these
checks, plus split integrity, seen-versus-unseen companies, the between-company
share of prediction variance and company-clustered significance, on any file with
one row per (call, ticker, date, split, horizon, truth, prediction). A published
result can therefore be checked from the one file its authors already have.

Reproductions and post-cutoff tests. 
Of seventeen papers in this line, one releases code that runs as released
(Same-Company-Same-Signal), seven release code that needs repairs or inputs that
were never shipped, and nine release nothing usable (Table N).
We rerun the five with usable code (HTML, KeFVP, Same-Company-Same-Signal,
[ref], DialogueGAT) on their own data, using the authors'
code wherever it can be made to start and a line-by-line port where it cannot.
Every patch is in a released ledger. We first compare with the published number
and then rerun the same model under a gapped split and a ticker-disjoint split
of the same data. Earnings25 allows two more tests, namely the frozen Stage-3
audio models rerun by bitrate, and the whole FinCall-trained pipeline scored,
with no retraining, on calls that postdate every model's training cutoff.

===== 09-results-a =====
The tables in this section use FinCall-Surprise, the v target and the
trading-day definition. MAEC, the level target, MSE, the calendar-day definition
and every other model are in Appendix N and in the supplement,
regenerated from the released CSVs.

The baseline to beat. 
HAR-RV is strong everywhere except one cell (Table N). On the FinCall
temporal split at =30, the training period includes the COVID spike and
the test year (2021) is calm, so every long-horizon forecast of the volatility
level, ours and every content model, does worse than persistence. The same models
do fine on the ticker-disjoint split and on MAEC, so we treat this as a regime
effect and note it. On the measured anchor a constant (the training mean) already
scores 0.17, 0.16, 0.06 and 0.09 on the temporal split and 0.20, 0.20, 0.13 and
0.04 on the ticker-disjoint split. That is the reference for every v
claim below.

Text models read the company, not the call (Table N
The text-only ridge scores about the same as the constant on both splits and never
comes within 0.09 of HAR-RV. The MLPs overfit. Adding text features to the
past-volatility ridge makes it worse in all eight cells. A linear probe
recovers the ticker from the frozen call embedding 89.5% of the time on FinCall
and 53.9% on MAEC. On the temporal split, where test companies also appear in
training, giving each test call the transcript of another call by the same
company changes the score by at most 0.02, while a random transcript hurts. The
model is reading which company is speaking. On the ticker-disjoint split the
same-company swap does hurt at 15, so there is some call-specific signal
for companies the model has never seen, but it stays below the past-volatility
baseline and disappears once past volatility is in the model (0.301 against 0.318
at =30). MAEC shows the same pattern.

===== 10-results-b =====
Audio adds nothing to past volatility. 
Audio-only ridges on eGeMAPS, WavLM-Large and emotion2vec+ stay within 0.10 of the
constant at every horizon on both splits and never beat it by more than 0.02.
Adding WavLM to the past-volatility ridge makes it worse in all eight cells. In the
audio-swap check (Table N, bottom), the model with past volatility
keeps its score at =3 when every test call is given a random other
company's audio (0.456 to 0.453). At longer horizons the swap costs more, but the
model with real audio is still below the ridge with past volatility alone, so
whatever the audio contributes does not add to the baseline. A probe still
recovers the ticker from pooled WavLM embeddings 76.3% of the time. A gender
check (dominant-speaker pitch proxy) finds no error gap (test MSE 0.243 against
0.239). Rerun on Earnings25 by bitrate, the gain from real over randomly swapped
audio never exceeds 0.03, is negative in 7 of 12 cells, and is no larger at
64 kbps than at lower bitrates (Appendix Table N). The
FinCall result is not an artefact of its 40 kbps audio.

Combining modalities. 
Gated fusion is worse than its inputs everywhere. The stacking model is the best
content model but stays below HAR-RV in six of eight cells. It beats HAR-RV only in
the COVID cell (0.100, still below the past-volatility ridge at 0.113) and at
ticker-disjoint =30 (0.396 against 0.345, Holm p=0.003 against
Stage 1, with no pre-registered test against HAR-RV). It beats the text and audio
stages in seven of eight cells but not the past-volatility stage. So a multimodal
model is not better than the best single-input model when that input is past
volatility, and the condition we set in advance for a positive paper (gains
surviving the checks on at least two of four horizons) was not met.

===== 11-results-c =====
The field's own models (Table N
Among the five papers we could run, the results that reproduce belong to
price-only or same-company models (Appendix N).
Same-Company-Same-Signal reproduces from its shipped data. TSMixer matches to
10-5 and TMLP within 0.02 (including their own within-company swap result),
and its training-free same-company history comes within 0.05 of KeFVP's published
MSE at every horizon on EC and MAEC-15 with no text and no training, a model that
has no meaning once companies are held out. KeFVP, a price-series model
conditioned on text with its audio branch disabled in its own code, reproduces
seven of eight MAEC cells within one published standard deviation. Its EC row, run
without the knowledge-enhanced embeddings that are no longer available, is above
all four published cells and we do not count it. HTML's released driver shuffles
features and labels independently without a seed, so it cannot have produced the
published table, and with the authors' model classes unchanged the published
recipe underfits at 15 on every split. The published ensemble of
[ref] can be reached only because its untrained text and audio
branches act as an intercept on a finance SVR whose mixing weights the code tunes
on the test set. Under our checks (Table N), holding companies
out costs the three text models 12-82% in MSE while TSMixer and KeFVP do not
move, although their training sets are as large (EC) or cut by the same third
(DEC, MAEC). The gap between training and test moves models by at most 13%.
HTML's own trained model changes by at most 4% when a test transcript is swapped
for another call by the same company, against 9-23% for a different company's
call. What HTML reads from a transcript is which company is speaking.

===== 12-results-d =====
After the cutoff (Table N
We scored the FinCall-trained models, unchanged, on 475 Earnings25 calls from late
2025, after every model's training cutoff and on companies none had seen. On the
level target, no model that was competitive before the cutoff got worse. HAR-RV
rose from 0.44, 0.37, 0.14 and -0.29 to 0.60, 0.54, 0.35 and 0.08, and the
content ridges rose with it (only the fusion ridge is unstable, at =30).
Levels are not comparable across the cutoff (different years and companies), but
the ordering is, and it holds. HAR-RV beats every content model at every horizon,
and no content model that was competitive before the cutoff collapses after it.
Under the call-date anchor of the published label sets, the same frozen models
collapse before the cutoff (text plus past volatility on v falls to
between -1.7 and -3.6, Appendix Table N), because
the reaction session then sits inside the past-volatility features.

===== 13-discussion =====
Four things hold across the whole set of models. Classical volatility forecasts
are hard to beat, and measuring against persistence exposes a regime failure that
raw MSE hides. Text features add nothing to past volatility, because the
embeddings identify the company, and where companies recur in training, swapping
in another call by the same company changes nothing. Audio features add nothing
either, at 40 kbps or 64 kbps, even though they too identify the company, and
combining them does not help. None of this could be seen with the files the field
has been using. The same checks on the field's own code and data give the same
answer. What reproduces is price-only models or same-company history, and the
text models lose their edge as soon as the company is new. The strongest recent
positive results [ref] report no identity checks and
release no code. The audit predictions command makes the check cheap. We
do not claim that transcripts and audio carry no information about a company's
risk. We claim that the benchmarks in use cannot tell that information apart from
knowing which company is speaking, and that once they can, nothing yet built
beats a forecast from past volatility.

===== 14-limitations =====
One post-cutoff quarter. The post-cutoff test rests on 475 calls from a
single calm quarter and a different universe from the training corpus, so it
bounds contamination in the ordering of models, not in their absolute scores.
Reproductions run on their own data, not on ecvol-bench, to keep "the
model fails" separable from "the model does not transfer". Two carry known
costs. KeFVP's released EC embeddings are unavailable upstream, and DialogueGAT's
corpus was never released, so our port stops at its protocol. MLP heads
are seed-unstable (standard deviation of R2 across seeds up to 6.8 on the
level target and 1.1 on v), which is why the grid uses deterministic
models. MLP cells in the appendix are seed means with that caveat.
Short-horizon R2 inflation. The persistence denominator is noisiest
at =3 (its MSE is about 2.8 times that at =7), so short-horizon
gains are easier to show. Timestamps. 10.7% of FinCall and 31.4% of
MAEC calls have no Item 2.02 filing in the window and keep a flagged after-hours
fallback, and intraday releases are treated as after-hours. Results under the
call-date anchor of prior label sets are not interchangeable with ours, and the
call-date targets are shipped as a variant. Regime sensitivity.
FinCall-Surprise spans COVID. The temporal =30 failure shows that
long-horizon level forecasts are regime-sensitive, which is why we emphasise
 v and per-year breakdowns. MAEC coverage. Its 78.6% price-join
rate (2015-2018 delistings) makes it a secondary corpus, and it ships no raw
audio. Source audio. Every FinCall file is a 40 kbps MP3 transcode and
Earnings25 is 64 kbps or lower, so no result here is established on lossless
audio. No diarisation. Audio is pooled per call, and the gender analysis
uses a dominant-speaker pitch proxy. Identity reconstruction is
imperfect. 7.1% of FinCall calls remain unresolved and are excluded with a
reason code.
