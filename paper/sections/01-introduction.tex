\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Earnings calls are the one recurring event at which management speaks at length,
semi-spontaneously, about a public company, in both \emph{what} is said and
\emph{how}. From \citet{qin2019what} through HTML \citep{yang2020html}, MAEC
\citep{li2020maec}, VolTAGE \citep{sawhney2020voltage}, DialogueGAT
\citep{sang2022dialoguegat}, KeFVP \citep{niu2023kefvp} and ECC Analyzer
\citep{cao2024ecc}, a lineage of multimodal models reports beating classical
methods at predicting the 3--30-day log realised volatility that follows a call.
Two observations undercut that progress: \citet{scss2024} show that transcript
representations mainly encode \emph{which company} is speaking and that
training-free baselines on a company's own volatility history beat every transcript
model, and only $\sim$14\% of reported financial-NLP results reproduce
\citep{finnlp2025survey}.

We argue both are properties of the \emph{evaluation substrate} the field shares,
and that it can be repaired. Nearly every paper above scores on one physical set
of label and split files passed from paper to paper. Those files have no embargo
between training and test although targets run 30 days past each call; most test
calls come from companies in training, and no paper reports the disjoint
condition; they carry degenerate cells; they encode a calendar-day horizon that
downstream work describes as trading days; and they anchor day~0 on the call date
although, as we measure from EDGAR, the earnings release precedes the market open
for most calls. The open corpus with raw audio, FinCall-Surprise
\citep{fincall2025}, ships with no ticker, date or speaker metadata, so the identity
controls that would expose the confound cannot be run on it; its successor
MM-FinEval \citep{mmfineval2026} adds ticker and date fields but no embargoed or
company-disjoint split, past-volatility baseline, or identity control.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~\textbf{\ecvol-bench}: identity and dates reconstructed for FinCall-Surprise
(92.9\% of calls, 60/60 manual audit), release timestamps measured from 8-K
filings, three target families under both horizon conventions, embargoed temporal
and \tickerdisjoint{} splits, SHA-256 manifests, and a harness in which every table
regenerates from one command (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}). (2)~\textbf{A substrate
audit} of the shared label and split files (Table~\ref{tab:substrate}).
(3)~\textbf{An identity-control suite} (ticker probes, same-company and global
content shuffles, \tickerdisjoint{} evaluation), released as a command that audits
\emph{any} prediction file. (4)~\textbf{What the controls show} for modern
open-weight text and audio representations against strong econometric baselines
(\S\ref{sec:results}). (5)~\textbf{A reproduction study} of the lineage's five
runnable models on their own data under the same controls, with a code-availability
audit of seventeen papers. (6)~\textbf{Post-cutoff evaluation} of the frozen
pipeline on 475 calls from late 2025. Stages, controls and confirmatory tests were
pre-registered in a public decision log, including the positive-framing gate the
content models missed.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Known pitfalls organise the design: ticker-identity leakage \citep{scss2024},
which LLMs reproduce even on entity-masked transcripts \citep{memorization2025};
LLM lookahead bias, with diagnostics and cutoff-bounded models
\citep{lookahead2025,datedgpt2026,lookaheadbench2026} but no frozen earnings-call
pipeline evaluated post-cutoff; weak baselines, where persistence, GARCH and HAR-RV
\citep{corsi2009har} are hard to beat and persistence is the natural denominator
\citep{gukellyxiu2020}; and gender bias in audio features \citep{ama2024,emobias2024}.
\citet{fastnumbers2026} time releases from 8-Ks for return prediction; no prior
work audits the volatility label sets. The strongest recent positive claims
\citep{soundofrisk2025,cao2024ecc} report no identity controls and release no code.
