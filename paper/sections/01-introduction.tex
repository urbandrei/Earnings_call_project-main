\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An earnings call is the one regular occasion on which a company's managers speak
at length about their business. Since \citet{qin2019what}, a line of papers has
used both the transcript and the audio of these calls to predict how volatile the
stock will be over the following 3 to 30 trading days, and HTML
\citep{yang2020html}, MAEC \citep{li2020maec}, VolTAGE \citep{sawhney2020voltage},
DialogueGAT \citep{sang2022dialoguegat}, KeFVP \citep{niu2023kefvp} and ECC
Analyzer \citep{cao2024ecc} all report beating classical forecasts.
\citet{scss2024} gave a reason to look again. On a corpus of their own, transcript representations mostly told the model {\it which company} was speaking, and on that corpus and on the shared benchmarks a forecast from the company's own past volatility matched or beat every transcript
model.

What that leaves open is whether any published model keeps its score
once the company is taken away, and the files the field scores on
cannot answer it. Nearly every paper in this line uses one set of label
and split files, handed down from paper to paper.  We audited them (\S\ref{sec:substrate}). They leave no gap between
training and test dates even though each target runs 30 days past its call. Most
test calls come from companies that are also in training, and no paper reports
what happens when they are not. They define horizons in calendar days, which later
papers describe as trading days. They put day 0 on the date of the call, although most companies publish before the open, a defect  noted by \citet{scss2024} which no shared label set corrects. Their
labels cannot be regenerated from prices under any single stated rule. The only
open corpus with raw audio, FinCall-Surprise \citep{fincall2025}, has no ticker,
date or speaker fields, so the identity checks cannot be run on it at all. No
published model had been rerun under such checks, and none had been scored on
calls after its training cutoff. Figure~\ref{fig:problems} shows the two
properties that matter most. This paper makes the diagnosis measurable on the
files in use and releases the tools for it.

\paragraph{What we contribute.}
(1)~\textbf{An evaluation protocol}, released as \ecvol-bench. We recover company
identities and dates for FinCall-Surprise (92.9\% of calls, and a 60-call manual
audit passes 60/60) and measure release times from 8-K filings. The release ships
three target families under both horizon definitions, splits with a 30-session
gap and company-disjoint splits, checksums for every file, and a harness in which
every table comes from one command (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}). It redistributes no
raw audio or transcripts. What it adds to the existing corpora is the identity
and timing tables, the targets, splits and derived features, and the harness.
(2)~\textbf{An audit} of the shared label and split files
(Table~\ref{tab:substrate}). (3)~\textbf{An audit toolkit.} Identity checks
(ticker probes, same-company and random content swaps, company-disjoint
evaluation) released as a command that works on \emph{any} prediction file.
(4)~\textbf{Results} for modern open-weight text and audio models against strong
baselines (\S\ref{sec:results}). (5)~\textbf{A reproduction study} of the five
runnable models in this line, on their own data, with the same checks, plus a
code-availability audit of seventeen papers. (6)~\textbf{A post-cutoff test} of
the frozen pipeline on 475 calls from late 2025. Stages, checks and confirmatory
tests were recorded in a dated decision log, released with the protocol, before
the corresponding results existed.
